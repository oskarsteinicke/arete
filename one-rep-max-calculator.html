How it's worked out

This uses the Epley formula, one of the most common ways to estimate a max from a set of several reps:

One rep max = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)

A single rep counts as itself. So 100 kg for 5 reps estimates a max of about 117 kg, and 80 kg for 10 estimates about 107 kg.

The table under your result works backwards: each percentage of your max, and roughly how many reps you could do with it.

How accurate is it

It's closest for sets of about 2 to 10 reps, done close to failure. The more reps, the looser it gets, because how many reps people can do at a given percentage varies a lot. Someone built for endurance gets more reps out of 80% than someone built for power. Expect a real max test to land a few percent either side of the estimate.

Using the percentages

  • 85% and up, 1 to 5 reps: the usual range for building maximum strength.
  • 65% to 85%, 6 to 12 reps: the usual range for building muscle.
  • Below 65%: warm-up sets, technique work and higher-rep sets.

Most programs, like 5/3/1 or a percentage-based powerlifting block, are written as percentages of your max. An estimate is a safe way to get that number without testing a true max.

Should you test a real max

Not often. A true one rep max is hard on the body and needs a spotter or safety bars. For tracking progress, an estimate from your normal working sets tells you just as much. If a lift starts to hurt, stop and get it looked at by a doctor or physio.
